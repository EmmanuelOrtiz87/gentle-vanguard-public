## Slide 3 — Three ways to bring an operation back in order

**Layout:** cards · **Time:** 1:10

**What to say:**
> There are three ways a project usually fits. The first is custom software: an application or a portal built around your processes, and also the evolution of a system that no longer scales. The second is automation: connecting what today lives in spreadsheets, in a CRM and in a payments system, and putting dashboards in place so tracking stops depending on someone reading a spreadsheet. The third is applied AI, and here we are careful: first we assess where artificial intelligence actually pays off, and then we build assistants connected to the organisation's own information, with clear limits and human review defined. We do not sell AI: we use it where it helps.

**Transition:** That is what we do. Now, how we do it, which is what usually worries the client more.

**Expected questions:**
- **Q:** How do you know whether AI adds value?
  **R:** Because AI comes after the process is understood. We first define the task to be solved and what a human would do; if rules or integration solve it, that is how we build it.
- **Q:** Do you also integrate systems we already have?
  **R:** Yes, that is part of automation work: the value usually lies in connecting what already exists, not in replacing everything.

**Do not say:**

## Slide 4 — A short method, with the criteria agreed before we start

**Layout:** principle · **Time:** 1:15

**What to say:**
> The method has three steps and it resembles any serious project, with one difference: the criteria are agreed before any code is written. First we understand: the problem, who uses it, the tools already in use. Then we build: priorities, stages, deliverables and acceptance criteria, and the first version is tested with the people who will use it. And then we stay with it: go-live, documentation, training and an agreed path for evolution. A project does not start with a proposal. It starts with a diagnostic that ends in a process map, the main pain points, the automation opportunities and a staged roadmap.

**Transition:** With the method clear, the logical question is what makes the result trustworthy.

**Expected questions:**
- **Q:** What happens if the scope changes halfway through?
  **R:** That is exactly what gets agreed in writing: stages with deliverables and acceptance criteria. A scope change is quoted as a new stage, not added to the original price.
- **Q:** Who owns the code?
  **R:** The client. The code, the documentation and the delivery criteria stay under the client's control.

**Do not say:**
- Do not say the method guarantees the project will go well: it bounds the risk, it does not remove it.
- Do not promise a fixed number of stages: it depends on the agreed scope.

## Slide 6 — A layered stack, not a one-off project

**Layout:** figureLayers · **Time:** 1:15

**What to say:**
> The stack has layers and each layer solves one class of problems. At the base, the tools layer: the CLI, the IDEs and the surfaces where the team works. Above it, orchestration: context, routing and steps. Then the agents, specialized profiles per runtime. In parallel, knowledge: Engram for memory, CodeGraph for the code index and Nexus, the operational database. And on top, quality and delivery: the gates, the review and the evidence. Three things are worth highlighting: there is real traceability, with sessions, traces, events and hash-chained audit; there is governance, with fail-closed policies and secret scanning; and it is local first: it runs on your team machine and is exposed to the cloud only when needed.

**Transition:** With the architecture clear, let us go back to the business: what a project looks like.

**Expected questions:**
- **Q:** What does "fail-closed" mean?
  **R:** That when a policy cannot be evaluated, the system blocks instead of letting it through. It is a design decision: we prefer to stop rather than let something improper pass.
- **Q:** Does our information leave your machine?
  **R:** Not by default. The stack runs locally; cloud exposure is optional and reversible.

**Do not say:**
- Do not list layers without saying what each one is for: the slide holds on the relationship, not the list.
- Do not say the client's data sits on our servers: it does not.

## Slide 9 — A studio with its own stack, versus an isolated build

**Layout:** compare · **Time:** 1:20

**What to say:**
> Let us compare two paths that often look equivalent. On the left, a studio operating on its own platform: fixed prices per milestone and bounded scope before we start, a documented diagnostic, code, documentation and delivery criteria under one quality bar, capabilities reused across projects, continuity after delivery and our own training. On the right, an isolated build: scope adjusted on the fly with cost as the variable, software handed over with no documented process and no transferred operation, every project re-solving the same base problems, dependence on a single person with no knowledge transfer, and a system that ages with no path forward. The real difference is not price: it is what is left working and operable when the project ends.

**Transition:** And there is something else that stays after every project.

**Expected questions:**
- **Q:** What hurts you most in that right column?
  **R:** The knowledge transfer. If the system depends on a single person, the client does not have a system: it has a contact.
- **Q:** What does "reused capabilities" mean?
  **R:** That a problem already solved in an earlier project is not charged for again in the next one.

**Do not say:**
- Do not say an isolated build "always" fails: it is the pattern we see, not a law.
- Do not attack other providers by name.
